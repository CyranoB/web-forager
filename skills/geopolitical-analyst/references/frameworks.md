# Framework selection guide

Use this catalogue to select lenses, not to display every available framework. Account for a framework's limits, but surface them only when they affect the conclusion.

## International-relations theories

| Framework | Best used for | Central question | Common failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical realism | Leadership, prudence, interests, power politics | How do power and political judgment shape conduct? | Reducing all motives to power |
| Structural realism | Polarity, balancing, alliance structure | What pressures follow from the distribution of capabilities? | Ignoring domestic politics and ideas |
| Neoclassical realism | Systemic pressure filtered through the state | How do leaders and state capacity translate pressure into policy? | Explaining outcomes after the fact |
| Liberal institutionalism | Institutions, cooperation, regimes | How do rules, information, and repeated interaction enable cooperation? | Underestimating coercion and distributional conflict |
| Complex interdependence | Networks, trade, finance, asymmetric dependencies | Who depends on whom, through which channels, and at what cost? | Assuming interdependence is pacifying |
| Constructivism | Identity, norms, legitimacy, role conceptions | How do shared meanings define interests and acceptable action? | Weakly specifying when ideas outweigh material incentives |
| English School | International order, sovereignty, legitimacy | How do power and a society of states jointly sustain order? | Treating contested norms as settled |
| Marxist or dependency approaches | Core-periphery relations, class, extraction | How do capital and unequal structures distribute gains and vulnerability? | Underweighting security and state autonomy |
| Feminist security analysis | Gendered power, human security, hidden labor | Whose security counts and whose costs are obscured? | Using gender as an add-on rather than a causal lens |
| Postcolonial analysis | Imperial legacies, hierarchy, knowledge production | How do colonial structures and categories persist? | Treating historical legacy as a complete explanation |

## Strategic and conflict frameworks

| Framework | Best used for | Central question | Common failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance of power | Alliances and counter-coalitions | How does the distribution of capabilities create balancing pressure? | Assuming balancing is automatic or timely |
| Balance of threat | Alliance choice and threat perception | How do power, proximity, offensive capability, and perceived intentions shape alignment? | Treating perceived intentions as directly observable |
| Security dilemma | Reciprocal fear and arms competition | Can defensive measures appear offensive and create spirals? | Applying it when one actor has clearly revisionist aims |
| Offense-defense balance | Military technology and posture | Is conquest perceived as easy, and can defensive intent be distinguished? | Treating the balance as objectively measurable |
| Deterrence and compellence | Threats, red lines, escalation | Are capability, credibility, communication, and resolve sufficient? | Assuming more threats always increase credibility |
| Bargaining model of war | War onset and settlement | Do information problems, commitment problems, or indivisibility block a bargain? | Abstracting away ideology and emotion |
| Alliance dilemma | Abandonment, entrapment, burden sharing | How does an ally manage dependence without losing autonomy? | Treating alliances as unitary actors |
| Power-transition theory | Rising powers and hegemonic rivalry | Does changing relative power interact with dissatisfaction with the order? | Turning transition into an inevitable-war claim |
| Two-level games | Diplomacy constrained by domestic politics | What agreements are acceptable internationally and ratifiable domestically? | Oversimplifying domestic coalitions |
| Strategic culture | Persistent patterns in threat perception and force | How do institutions and historical memory shape strategic preferences? | National stereotyping or circular reasoning |
| Organizational process / bureaucratic politics | Crisis decisions and implementation | How do routines, agencies, and bargaining shape policy? | Losing sight of executive authority and structure |

## Geography, regions, and political economy

| Framework | Best used for | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Regional Security Complex Theory | Regionally clustered threats and security interdependence | Define the region empirically; do not assume formal borders determine it |
| Geoeconomics / economic statecraft | Sanctions, trade, investment, finance, technology controls | Analyze substitution, enforcement, third-country effects, and blowback |
| Critical geopolitics | Maps, spatial narratives, identity, geopolitical discourse | Use alongside material evidence; discourse alone may not explain behavior |
| Sea-power traditions (Mahan) | Maritime access, fleets, trade routes, bases | Treat as a historical lens, not a timeless law |
| Heartland / rimland traditions (Mackinder, Spykman) | Continental access, buffers, transport geography | Avoid geographic determinism and account for technological change |
| Resource and chokepoint analysis | Energy, minerals, water, ports, cables, straits | Distinguish physical concentration from actual market or strategic leverage |
| State capacity and political settlements | Implementation, coercion, taxation, elite bargains | Avoid reducing legitimacy to coercive capacity |

## Structured analytic tools

These organize evidence but do not independently explain causation:

- **Actor-interest-capability-constraint map:** Compare what actors want, can do, cannot absorb, and depend upon.
- **DIME:** Review diplomatic, informational, military, and economic instruments.
- **PMESII(-PT):** Scan political, military, economic, social, information, infrastructure, physical environment, and time dimensions.
- **Center-of-gravity analysis:** Test critical capabilities, requirements, and vulnerabilities; avoid forcing a single decisive center.
- **Alternative futures / scenario axes:** Explore uncertainty through causal pathways and signposts.
- **Analysis of competing hypotheses:** Compare explanations against diagnostic evidence and seek disconfirmation.
- **Red teaming:** Model how another actor may perceive and exploit the situation without caricaturing it.

## Selection heuristic

1. Identify the causal puzzle.
2. Choose the lens with the best explanatory fit.
3. Add a lens with different assumptions or a different level of analysis.
4. Add an applied lens or structured tool only if it exposes a missing variable or supports comparison.
5. Drop any lens that merely restates facts or repeats another framework's conclusion.

Framework convergence does not prove a conclusion. Check for shared assumptions and dependence on the same uncertain evidence.
