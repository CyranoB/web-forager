# Evidence and source standards

## Match the source to the claim

- Use original legal texts, treaties, judgments, official records, budgets, statistical releases, sanctions notices, filings, and primary data for what those documents directly establish.
- Use statements from governments, organizations, parties, militaries, or companies as evidence of their stated position, not automatically of underlying fact or intent.
- Use credible local, regional, and international reporting for current events, witness accounts, and independently developed investigations. Examine sourcing and corrections practices.
- Use peer-reviewed research and established scholarship for theory, historical comparison, and causal findings. Check whether the evidence travels to the present case.
- Use specialist institutes, monitoring organizations, and domain experts when their methods, data, and incentives can be assessed.
- Treat aggregators, commentary, anonymous claims, and social posts as leads or evidence of discourse unless independently verified.

State media, advocacy groups, intelligence-linked outlets, governments, companies, and think tanks can supply useful evidence while retaining institutional incentives. Describe the relevant limitation rather than accepting or rejecting a source by category.

## Triangulate and stop

- Seek independent evidentiary chains, not repeated publication of one wire report, press release, anonymous source, or original artifact.
- For disputed high-impact claims, seek two independent credible chains where feasible and examine the strongest contrary evidence.
- Compare statements with observable behavior, primary documents, data, imagery, or later corrections.
- Use local-language sources when material and accessible. Disclose machine translation or limited language access when it could affect interpretation.
- Treat absence of evidence cautiously under censorship, war, restricted access, or low state capacity.
- Stop when added sources repeat existing chains and no longer change the assessment or confidence.

## Verify visual and open-source evidence

For images, video, satellite imagery, social posts, or other OSINT, check provenance, original upload, capture time, location, editing or synthetic-media risk, and consistency with independent evidence. Do not count reports that reuse the same artifact as independent confirmation. State material verification limits.

## Handle legal and atrocity claims

Distinguish allegations, documented evidence, official designations, investigative findings, and judicial determinations. Attribute contested legal characterizations. Use terms such as genocide, war crime, crime against humanity, aggression, occupation, and terrorism according to the relevant legal or institutional basis; do not present an analyst's strategic inference as a judicial finding.

## Warn about claims selectively

Do not label well-supported claims. Warn only when an evidence problem affects a material claim:

- **Unverified:** Independent confirmation is insufficient. This is not a finding of falsity.
- **Unsupported or unfounded:** Available evidence does not substantiate the claim.
- **Misleading:** Missing context materially changes the implication of otherwise defensible wording.
- **Contradicted:** Stronger evidence supports a different conclusion.
- **False:** Strong, relevant evidence clearly disproves the claim. Reserve this label for high-confidence cases.

Do not label a future prediction false or contradicted solely because current evidence makes it unlikely. Use unverified, unsupported, or a calibrated likelihood assessment unless a factual premise is contradicted or the forecast horizon has passed.

Attribute the claim, cite the probative evidence, and state what remains uncertain. Do not infer motive from error alone.

## Maintain recency and citation integrity

- Record the research cutoff when browsing or recency matters.
- Distinguish the event date from the publication date.
- Prefer the latest authoritative release for changing figures and preserve its measurement period.
- Link to the specific document or page supporting the claim, not a search result or generic homepage.
- Place citations near consequential claims. Make clear when several sentences rely on one source.
- Do not cite a source not opened or inspected. Do not treat a headline as the underlying evidence.

## Calibrate confidence

Use **high**, **moderate**, or **low** confidence for material inferences and forecasts:

- High: strong, relevant, largely independent evidence with limited sensitivity to plausible assumptions.
- Moderate: credible evidence with meaningful gaps, disagreement, or dependence on interpretation.
- Low: sparse, contested, indirect, rapidly changing, or assumption-sensitive evidence.

Confidence describes support for the assessment, not the severity or probability of an outcome.

## Audit articles proportionately

Test the most consequential claims, prioritizing those that are central, surprising, disputed, quantitative, causal, or predictive. Check whether cited sources support the wording. Examine omitted base rates, denominators, time windows, counterexamples, and alternative causal explanations. Use a claim table only when comparison improves clarity or the user requests a comprehensive audit.
